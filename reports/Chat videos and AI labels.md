# Canva's AI clips, not Kokoro, trigger the labels

Short answer: build the chat-story format if you want a new format, but do not build it to escape AI labels, because the labels are not coming from the Kokoro voice. A check of our own exported files on 27 Oct 2026 found that the five-concepts videos, which contain AI-generated Ledger clips made in Canva, carry a signed C2PA "Content Credentials" manifest naming **"Canva AI"** as the software and **`compositeWithTrainedAlgorithmicMedia`** as the source type. Meta, TikTok, YouTube and LinkedIn all read that signal and can label automatically. The Kokoro-voiced explainer reels, exported through the same Canva music step, carry **no manifest at all**. So the fix for the "AI problem" is to stop putting Canva AI media into videos, which costs nothing, rather than to build software. Even where a label does appear, the platforms say it is not used for ranking, and the best study finds a small cost of about **7% fewer likes per view**, not a collapse in reach. The chat format is worth a test on its own merits: it costs about 1-2 days to add to the existing renderer, it needs no AI to make, and it is legally safe as long as the characters are obviously fictional, nobody praises Edlintics or reports results from using it, and the interface is our own design, not a copy of iMessage, WhatsApp, Slack or ChatGPT. The reel Hoang shared, @orbi_the_alien's "Corporate Life in 2026" series, is the right model once its realistic photo avatars and copied app screens are swapped for illustrated avatars and an Edlintics design: start a voice-free series called "Month-end in the group chat · part 1" with Ledger as one of the users. Two red lines apply: "fictional users" must only ever mean characters inside a video, never fake accounts that post, like or comment, and no character may resemble a real student

## What to do next, in order

| Step | What | Why |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Stop using Canva AI or AI-generated media (Magic Media, Dream Lab, AI video) inside any post design. Rebuild future five-concepts Ledger clips in our own explainer-reel engine | This is the only source of an embedded AI manifest we found. On YouTube, a label applied from C2PA cannot be reversed |

| 6 | Model the first episodes on the @orbi_the_alien "Corporate Life in 2026" reel Hoang shared: a voice-free series called "Month-end in the group chat · part 1", Ledger as one user, coworkers labelled by role ("Priya · Controller"), illustrated avatars, and our own chat and assistant screens instead of Slack, Teams or ChatGPT look-alikes | The reference reel's satire, mascot-as-user and "part N" hook fit our office comics; its realistic photo avatars and copied app screens are the two parts to leave out |

## A Canva manifest, not the voice, explains the labels

All the major platforms tie a *mandatory* creator label to realism. YouTube requires disclosure when content "makes a real person appear to say or do something they didn't do" or "generates a realistic scene that didn't actually occur", and explicitly exempts fully animated video and clearly unrealistic content ([YouTube Help](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/14328491)). TikTok's guidelines, in the version in force since 24 Sep 2026, require labels on AI content "that shows realistic-looking scenes or people" and no longer mention audio ([TikTok Community Guidelines](https://www.tiktok.com/community-guidelines/en/integrity-authenticity)). Meta is the outlier: it requires disclosure for "photorealistic video or realistic-sounding audio that was digitally created or altered", with no carve-out for animation ([Meta Transparency Center](https://transparency.meta.com/policies/community-standards/manipulated-media/)). LinkedIn has no self-label rule and simply shows a "CR" badge when it finds C2PA credentials ([Fortune](https://fortune.com/2024/11/26/linkedin-content-credentials-labeling-challenges-c2pa)). X targets media that is "significantly and deceptively altered" ([X Help](https://help.twitter.com/en/rules-and-policies/manipulated-media)), and Threads follows Meta's approach ([Meta newsroom](https://about.fb.com/news/2024/04/metas-approach-to-labeling-ai-generated-content-and-manipulated-media/))

Automatic labels work differently. They fire mainly on **embedded provenance signals**, meaning C2PA credentials, IPTC "digital source type" metadata and invisible watermarks, plus each platform's own AI tools. YouTube labels content carrying C2PA metadata and content "its systems detect as AI-generated", and a label from C2PA or manual review cannot be changed by the creator ([YouTube Help](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/14328491)). TikTok has auto-labelled uploads carrying C2PA since May 2024 ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2024/05/09/tiktok-automatically-label-ai-generated-content-created-other-platforms)) and in November 2025 added its own invisible watermark to them ([TikTok Newsroom](https://newsroom.tiktok.com/more-ways-to-spot-shape-and-understand-ai-generated-content)). Meta labels AI-generated content found through "industry-shared signals" ([Meta newsroom](https://about.fb.com/news/2024/04/metas-approach-to-labeling-ai-generated-content-and-manipulated-media/))

That is why the local file check matters. Our five-concepts videos (economics, statistics, marketing, management, clips 2-6) contain AI-generated Ledger clips and carry a signed manifest declaring "Canva AI" and `compositeWithTrainedAlgorithmicMedia`. The eight Kokoro explainer reels (accruals, break-even, salesperson, working capital, marginal utility, diminishing returns, deskilling, Moravec) and the title and end cards of the five-concepts sets carry none. Canva therefore attaches its AI manifest only when the design contains Canva AI or AI-generated media, not on every export. Kokoro is an open-weight model with no documented watermark ([DeepInfra](https://deepinfra.com/hexgrad/Kokoro-82M)), and an ffmpeg render writes no AI provenance. The most likely explanation for the labels we have seen is the five-concepts AI clips, and a chat video built in our engine and finished in Canva without AI elements would carry nothing

| Platform | Creator must label when | Our five-concepts videos (Canva AI manifest) | Our Kokoro reels (no manifest) | Code-rendered chat videos |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| YouTube | Realistic people, scenes or events; animation exempt | Auto-label likely, and it cannot be reversed | No label required; a classifier flag is possible and reversible | No label |
| TikTok | Realistic-looking scenes or people | Auto-label likely | Optional under the 2026 wording | No label |
| Facebook, Instagram, Threads | Photorealistic video or realistic-sounding audio | Auto-label likely | Grey area because of the voice; turn on "AI info" | No label, unless a realistic AI voice is added |
| LinkedIn | No self-label rule | "CR" badge likely | Nothing | Nothing |
| X | Deceptive manipulated media | Low risk | Nothing | Nothing |

Some uncertainty remains. Meta has never published examples of what counts as "realistic-sounding audio", TikTok's own help page on AI labels could not be read, and the local scan covered the exports in one scratchpad folder, not every file ever posted. YouTube's classifier could also flag a synthetic voice on its own, though a classifier label can be reversed by answering "No" ([YouTube Help](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/14328491))

## A label costs about 7% of likes per view, not your reach

No platform says the label itself lowers ranking. YouTube states that "the algorithm won't be using the disclosure labels when determining recommendations or ranking" and that labels have no effect on monetisation ([YouTube Official Blog](https://blog.youtube/culture-and-trends/renes-top-five-on-youtube-march-21-2024-edition/)), and its help page repeats that disclosure "alone won't limit a video's audience" ([YouTube Help](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/14328491)). TikTok's help centre reportedly says the setting "won't affect the distribution of your video", but that quote is only available through third-party pages ([Storrito](https://storrito.com/resources/how-to-label-ai-content-on-tiktok-without-losing-reach/)). Meta has said nothing either way about the per-post label. What the platforms do punish is failing to disclose when disclosure is required, and mass-produced or templated content. Adam Mosseri summed up Instagram's line as "label AI content, verify real content, and continue improving ranking systems that reward originality" ([Netinfluencer](https://www.netinfluencer.com/instagram-launches-ai-creator-labels-to-flag-accounts-that-regularly-use-ai-generated-content/))

There is one indirect channel. Since November 2025 TikTok lets each viewer turn AI content down (not off) with a slider in "Manage topics" ([Social Media Today](https://www.socialmediatoday.com/news/tiktok-adds-option-to-limit-ai-content-in-your-for-you-feed/805952/)), so a labelled video can reach fewer of the people who chose less AI. Nobody has measured how large that effect is

The best audience evidence is a 2026 *Journal of Consumer Research* paper by Carney, Riveros and Tully. Across 1.1 million TikTok posts, posts carrying a disclosure got **about 7-8% fewer likes and about 7% less engagement per view**. In experiments the effect was small overall (d about 0.2-0.26) and larger among viewers who noticed the label (d about 0.45-0.63). It worked through perceived effort: viewers assumed the creator tried less and felt less connected to them. Disclosures that signalled effort shrank the penalty ([JCR](https://academic.oup.com/jcr/advance-article/doi/10.1093/jcr/ucag013/8672493)). Other studies point in different directions. A CHI 2025 study of 911 people found no significant change in liking, commenting or sharing ([Sherlock Comms summary](https://sherlockcomms.com/ai-content-labels-audience-reactions/)), while research on headlines found an "AI-generated" label lowered how accurate and shareable people judged even *true* content ([PNAS Nexus](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11443540)). That last point matters for an education brand, whose value rests on being believed

The voice itself is a smaller issue than it feels. In learning studies, high-quality AI narration performs about as well as human narration, and audio quality matters more than whether the voice is human ([TechSmith](https://techsmith.com/blog/ai-voices-avatars-in-training-videos); [ICCE 2025](https://library.apsce.net/index.php/ICCE/article/view/6068)). People still trust human voices more, though most of that data comes from vendor surveys ([Adobe Express](https://www.adobe.com/express/learn/blog/ai-audio-consumer-trust)). Taken together, the label is a modest cost worth managing with specific, effort-showing disclosure, not a crisis worth an engineering project

## Chat stories can hold attention, but nobody has proven them for teaching

The format can pull huge numbers. The best-documented case is a dating-reply app called RIZZ, whose network of 15+ TikTok accounts reportedly reached about **550M views at roughly 450K average views per video**, using 2-5 minute text stories over Minecraft gameplay with a light call to action at the end ([Shortimize](https://www.shortimize.com/blog/rizz-apps-b-roll-strategy-that-got-them-half-a-billion-views-on-tiktok)). That is a single vendor source, the stories are relationship drama, and the reach came from a multi-account network. The research found **no verified education, finance or business account** using chat stories as its main format, and no published retention data for the genre. Edlintics would have the niche to itself, with no proof yet that it teaches or converts

The reference reel Hoang shared shows a better-fitting version of the format. It is "part 2 · Corporate Life in 2026" from the verified account @orbi_the_alien, captioned "someone help meeee", with about 369 comments and 1,265 reposts when the coordinator viewed it in Chrome on 27 Oct 2026. A black 9:16 frame holds a fixed title at the top, and the middle band shows a screen recording of a Slack- or Teams-style work chat, with a mouse cursor and timestamps. The brand's cartoon alien mascot, Orbi, is one of the users, and the coworkers are labelled by role ("Ashley · Manager", "Tom · Consultant") with realistic photo avatars. Orbi's messages arrive one line at a time ("guys", "now it's time", "that we show the world", "that we are NOT"), a dozen consultants answer with a wall of identical "Yeah" replies, and the video cuts to a ChatGPT-like assistant screen holding "implementation_plan_Orbi.pptx". It is corporate satire run as a numbered series, and the high repost count suggests office workers share it because they recognise themselves

Three parts of that template transfer directly to Edlintics: a mascot as one chat user, workplace satire close to our office comics, and no voice at all, which keeps it outside every labelling rule above. The "part N" series numbering works as a hook and a reason to follow. Two parts should not be copied. The realistic photo avatars may be AI faces or stock photos, and AI faces of natural-looking fictional people fall under the EU AI Act's deepfake reading and Meta's realism rules, so Edlintics should use illustrated avatars in the Ledger style. The screens also imitate real products (Slack or Teams, and ChatGPT), so our version should use an original Edlintics chat and an original "assistant" screen with no real product's look or name. The suggested series is "Month-end in the group chat · part 1", with Ledger as one user and coworkers labelled by role, each episode ending on the concept it dramatised

The brand risk is real but avoidable. Gameplay-backed fake-text stories sit alongside Reddit-story text-to-speech in the "sludge" or "brainrot" genre ([Wikipedia: Sludge content](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sludge_content)), and the format is sold as a cheap money-making template ([Gumroad](https://dailystories.gumroad.com/l/e-book)). The brainrot signal comes from the gameplay background, robotic voices and clickbait drama, not from the chat interface. A clean, branded chat about a believable workplace moment reads like the office comics Edlintics already posts

Originality rules are the main platform risk. YouTube's monetisation policy names "templated storylines, or scrolling text with minimal or no narrative, commentary, or educational value" and "characters put in the same situation over and over again with the same outcome", while allowing a series where "each video has a distinct storyline, focus, or concept" ([YouTube monetization policies](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/1311392)). TikTok removes "unoriginal or reused material without anything new" from the For You feed ([TikTok Community Guidelines](https://www.tiktok.com/community-guidelines/en/integrity-authenticity)), and Meta downranks accounts that repost others' material ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2025/07/14/following-youtube-meta-announces-crackdown-on-unoriginal-facebook-content)). Original scripts inside one consistent design are not "reused", so the danger is sameness at volume. Each video needs a different concept and a real lesson at the end, gameplay footage should never be used, and chat stories should be a few a week mixed with other formats, not a daily template flood. Edlintics is not monetised on these platforms, so the cost of getting this wrong would be lost distribution, which matters more for an organic brand anyway

A practical spec follows from the evidence. Aim for 25-45 seconds, roughly 10-18 messages, about 17 characters per second of reading time, the subtitle standard ([Middlebury](https://www.middlebury.edu/institute/advancing-your-career/career-guide/top-three-video-subtitling-mistakes-beginners-make)). Open on the conflict ("Why does the P&L say we made $12,000 when the bank has $800?"), hold the reveal until late, and end on a 3-5 second definition card. Keep the chat inside the safe area, roughly x 120-840 and y 240-1500 at 1080x1920 ([Socialync](https://www.socialync.io/tools/safe-zones/tiktok)). A Kokoro voice reading the definition adds the "narration and educational value" that YouTube's wording rewards, but it brings back the Meta grey area, so either keep chat videos voice-free or turn on the toggle for the ones that use it

## Fiction is legal, fake praise is not

The legal line is whether viewers would read the chat as a real customer's opinion of Edlintics. In the US, the FTC rule bans reviews and testimonials from people who do not exist or never used the product, explicitly including AI-generated ones, and also bans fake social-media indicators ([FTC](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2024/08/federal-trade-commission-announces-final-rule-banning-fake-reviews-testimonials)). It also says that "if the television commercial is an obviously fictional dramatization, then the actors are not giving testimonials" ([FTC Q&A](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/consumer-reviews-testimonials-rule-questions-answers)). The Endorsement Guides give the same example of unidentified shoppers in a supermarket ad ([16 CFR 255.0](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/16/255.0)). In the EU, the Omnibus Directive added a blacklisted ban, meaning always unfair without further test, on submitting false consumer reviews or endorsements, and it applies whether or not the post is marked as an ad ([MakeInfluence](https://www.makeinfluence.com/en/academy/fake-and-manipulated-reviews-what-the-eu-omnibus-directive-bans)). A UK ruling shows a disclaimer cannot rescue a misleading overall impression: TikTok ads with actors posing as clients were banned because they "gave the overall impression that the testimonials were genuine" ([ASA](https://asa.org.uk/rulings/tms-legal-ltd-A23-1199376.html))

In practice this gives three zones. Safe: "Mia" and "Sam" work out why depreciation is not cash, and the video ends on the Edlintics logo, with nobody praising the course. Grey: a character says "I learned this on Edlintics", which reads as an experience claim, so it needs an on-screen (and spoken, if there is a voice) "Dramatization: fictional characters" and an obviously scripted style. Red: a realistic chat in which a "student" passes an exam or raises a grade thanks to the course. That works as a fake testimonial even with a disclaimer, and outcome claims should only ever use real, documented customer quotes given with consent

Copying real apps is a second, smaller risk. Apple forbids imitating its "distinctive... web site design, logos, or typefaces" ([Apple trademark guidelines](https://www.apple.com/legal/intellectual-property/guidelinesfor3rdparties.html)), and its ad policy says ads "can't mimic or resemble... the user interface of Apple products" ([Apple Ads](https://ads.apple.com/apple-news/publishers/help/content-guidelines/0060-general-content-guidelines)). WhatsApp's brand rules forbid implying partnership or using the brand in a misleading way ([Meta: WhatsApp brand](https://www.meta.com/brand/resources/whatsapp/whatsapp-brand/)). No enforcement against chat-video makers turned up, but an Edlintics-blue interface with our own bubble shapes, no app logos and no iOS status bar avoids the question entirely, and it also makes the fiction more obvious

Invented people are fine as long as they do not resemble real ones. Use illustrated avatars or initials, common first names only, and no surnames, schools or employers, and check names against the old client list kept under `data/contacts/` so no former student appears by accident. Avoid photorealistic AI faces. The EU AI Act's deepfake disclosure duty has applied to deployers since 2 Aug 2026, and the Commission's draft guidelines read it to cover a "realistic synthetic depiction of a fictitious but natural-looking person", with fines up to EUR 15 million or 3% of turnover ([Greenberg Traurig](https://gtlaw.com/en/insights/2026/6/deepfakes-chatbots-ai-generated-text-european-commission-details-transparency-obligations-under-the-ai-act)). A hand-drawn mascot with a generic narrator does not "resemble existing persons" and so is not a deepfake ([AI Act Art 50](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/50/)). One open question is whether self-hosting Kokoro makes Edlintics a "provider" with machine-readable marking duties from 2 Dec 2026 ([Hogan Lovells](https://www.hlc.com/en/publications/eu-legislators-agree-to-delay-for-highrisk-ai-rules)). It is unlikely to matter at our size, but it is worth a lawyer's view if the voice pipeline grows. A lawyer should also see any paid ad that makes outcome claims or uses real customers

## Build it into the existing engine instead of buying a tool

Off-the-shelf tools are cheap but built for the wrong thing. texting.video costs $9 a month, or $4.99 for a 24-hour pass, to remove its watermark ([texting.video](https://texting.video/pricing)), while AI "brainrot" suites run $20-$99 a month and bundle AI voices ([GhostShorts comparison](https://ghostshorts.com/best-texting-story-makers)). Every tool found is built around iMessage, WhatsApp, Instagram or Tinder skins, none offers a brand-owned design, none publishes clear commercial-use terms, and the bundled AI voices would bring back the very labelling question this project is meant to avoid. The open-source projects are unlicensed demos with unlicensed sound assets ([GitHub](https://github.com/samkwak188/Fake-Text-Video-Generator-Demo)), and Remotion would mean rewriting the engine in React plus a paid licence once Edlintics passes three employees ([Remotion licence](https://github.com/remotion-dev/remotion/blob/main/LICENSE.md))

Our renderer at `.claude/skills/edlintics-explainer-reel/scripts/render.js` already does the hard part. It seeks each frame with `window.render(t)` instead of letting animations play in real time, which avoids the frame-timing bugs other headless-browser tools warn about ([timecut](https://cdn.jsdelivr.net/npm/timecut@0.3.3/README.md); [HeyGen research](https://www.heygen.com/research/html-to-video)). A chat scene needs bubbles, typing dots, auto-scroll and a header with a Ledger avatar, all computed from `t`, with no CSS transitions and no embedded video. Sound effects slot into the existing mix with ffmpeg `adelay` at each message time. Make the "pop" and "typing" sounds ourselves, or use logged Mixkit or Pixabay files, which both allow commercial use ([Mixkit](https://mixkit.co/llm-info/); [Pixabay](https://pixabay.com/service/terms/)), and never use iOS system sounds. Bundle Noto Color Emoji (open font licence) if a character's message needs one, rather than Apple's system emoji ([Fontsource](https://fontsource.org/fonts/noto-color-emoji/about)). The estimate is about 1-2 days for a minimal version and 3-5 days for a polished one with read receipts, themes and a skill doc a cheaper model can follow. It has no recurring cost, and it keeps chat stories in the same script to build to Canva to hub-card flow as the other reels. If you want proof of audience interest before spending the days, a single texting.video day pass could test the idea, with the trade-off that the test video would wear an imitation skin

## Conclusion

The proposal rests on a misdiagnosis. The labels Hoang is reacting to come from Canva's AI provenance stamp on AI-generated clips, not from the voice and not from the mascot, so the cheapest fix is a production rule (no Canva AI media in posts, check every export before upload) rather than new software. That reframes the chat-story project from a workaround into an ordinary format bet, to be judged on whether it teaches and holds attention, and the honest answer there is that nobody knows yet for education content

The deeper lesson is that the "AI problem" is mostly a perception of effort, not a rule. The JCR evidence says audiences discount content that looks low-effort, and the originality policies say platforms do the same. Both point the same way: specific disclosure that shows the human work, a distinct concept in every video, and an interface and characters that are plainly Edlintics' own. A chat format built that way is safe. Built as a fast template of invented testimonials in borrowed iMessage skins, it would trade a minor labelling cost for real legal and reach risk
