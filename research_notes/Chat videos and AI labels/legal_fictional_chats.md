# Legal and platform rules for fictional chat conversations in Edlintics marketing videos

Scope: Edlintics, an EU-based business-education brand selling online courses to EU and non-EU customers, publishing videos that show fictional people chatting in a messaging interface. These are practical rules, not legal advice. Researched 27 Oct 2026.

## 1. Fake reviews and testimonials (FTC rule, FTC Endorsement Guides, EU UCPD and Omnibus, DSA)

### Takeaway
A fictional chat becomes a legal problem when viewers are likely to read it as a real customer's opinion or experience of Edlintics. A student character saying "this course got me through my exam" in a realistic, unlabeled chat is the riskiest case. A clearly fictional skit that teaches an accounting concept, with the brand only as publisher, is generally not an endorsement. Under US and UK rules, an "obviously fictional dramatization" is not a testimonial. If a character praises the course, label it on screen and audibly (for example "Dramatization. Fictional characters"), and never attach invented outcomes or numbers to the praise.

### Cited Findings
**US: FTC Consumer Reviews and Testimonials Rule (16 CFR Part 465, announced Aug 2024)**
- The rule bans creating, selling, buying or spreading reviews or testimonials that misrepresent who wrote them. That covers reviewers who do not exist, explicitly including "AI-generated fake reviews", and people who "did not have actual experience with the business or its products or services". It also applies to businesses that knew or should have known a review was fake. Other prohibitions cover paying for positive or negative sentiment, undisclosed insider reviews, company-controlled "independent" review sites, review suppression, and fake social-media indicators. The FTC can seek civil penalties for knowing violations — [FTC press release, Aug 2024](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2024/08/federal-trade-commission-announces-final-rule-banning-fake-reviews-testimonials)
- A "consumer testimonial" is "an advertising message that consumers are likely to believe reflects the opinions, beliefs, or experiences" of a consumer. It can appear in ads, websites and social media. A "consumer review" is an evaluation, or a "purported consumer's evaluation", posted on a platform dedicated to reviews — [FTC Q&A on the rule](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/consumer-reviews-testimonials-rule-questions-answers)
- Fictional dramatizations: "If the television commercial is an obviously fictional dramatization, then the actors are not giving testimonials." Actors portraying testimonialists fall under the rule only if the underlying testimonials are fake. Even so, using actors "could be deceptive in violation of the FTC Act" — [FTC Q&A](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/consumer-reviews-testimonials-rule-questions-answers)
- AI avatars: there is no blanket ban. An avatar is not a "consumer review" but could be a testimonial — [FTC Q&A](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/consumer-reviews-testimonials-rule-questions-answers)
- Disclosures must be "clear and conspicuous" and "unavoidable". A disclosure is avoidable if the viewer has to click or hover to see it. In video, "#Ad" may be too easy to miss, and platform disclosure tools are not automatically adequate, since they can be fleeting, small or low-contrast — [FTC Q&A](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/consumer-reviews-testimonials-rule-questions-answers)
- The Q&A states no dollar amount. A 2023 FTC notice of penalty offenses cited up to $43,792 per violation, a figure that is adjusted for inflation each year — [cappsonline summary of FTC](https://cappsonline.org/federal-trade-commission-announces-updated-advertising-guides-to-combat-deceptive-reviews-and-endorsements/)

**US: FTC Endorsement Guides (16 CFR 255)**
- An endorsement is "any advertising, marketing, or promotional message for a product that consumers are likely to believe" reflects the views of someone other than the advertiser. The endorser is the party whose views the message "appears to reflect", and it "could be or appear to be an individual" — [16 CFR 255.0 (Cornell LII)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/16/255.0)
- Example 2 (§ 255.0(g)(2)): two unidentified shoppers in a supermarket ad are an "obviously fictional dramatization" and "would not be an endorsement" — [16 CFR 255.0](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/16/255.0)
- Example 10 (§ 255.0(g)(10)): an app ad that shows people presented as real users discloses "via both audio and visual means that the persons featured are actors". Adequacy is judged from the target audience's perspective — [16 CFR 255.0](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/16/255.0)
- The 2023 revision brought fake reviews and virtual influencers into the definition of endorsement — [Crowell](https://www.crowell.com/en/insights/client-alerts/ftc-issues-long-awaited-updates-to-the-endorsement-guides); [cappsonline](https://cappsonline.org/federal-trade-commission-announces-updated-advertising-guides-to-combat-deceptive-reviews-and-endorsements/)

**EU: Unfair Commercial Practices Directive 2005/29/EC as amended by Omnibus Directive 2019/2161 (applies from 28 May 2022)**
- The Omnibus Directive added two blacklisted practices, which are always unfair and need no case-by-case test. Point 23b bans claiming reviews come from consumers who actually used or bought the product without "reasonable and proportionate steps" to check. Point 23c bans submitting, or commissioning another person to submit, false consumer reviews or endorsements, and misrepresenting reviews or social endorsements to promote products — [MakeInfluence summary](https://www.makeinfluence.com/en/academy/fake-and-manipulated-reviews-what-the-eu-omnibus-directive-bans); [EC TRIS notification text](https://technical-regulation-information-system.ec.europa.eu/cs/notification/26584/message/110913/EN); [Annex I text (ippt.eu)](https://www.ippt.eu/legal-texts/unfair-commercial-practices-directive/annex-1)
- The fake-review bans apply whether or not the content is labelled as an ad. Authenticity is a separate question from ad disclosure — [MakeInfluence](https://www.makeinfluence.com/en/academy/fake-and-manipulated-reviews-what-the-eu-omnibus-directive-bans)
- National transpositions vary in wording, for example Denmark's Annex 1 to its Marketing Practices Act — [MakeInfluence](https://www.makeinfluence.com/en/academy/fake-and-manipulated-reviews-what-the-eu-omnibus-directive-bans)

**UK (relevant if UK customers are targeted)**
- The DMCC Act 2024, Schedule 20, bans fake reviews and services that help write or suppress them — [legislation.gov.uk Sch 20](https://legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2024/13/schedule/20/data.html); [Anglesey Trading Standards](https://anglesey.gov.wales/en/business/trading-standards/trading-standards-guidance/fair-trading/331517)
- ASA ruling on TMS Legal (2023): TikTok ads used actors posing as paid clients. They were ruled misleading because they "gave the overall impression that the testimonials were genuine". The CAP Code requires evidence that a testimonial is genuine "unless it is obviously fictitious" — [ASA ruling](https://asa.org.uk/rulings/tms-legal-ltd-A23-1199376.html); [Legal Futures](https://legalfutures.co.uk/latest-news/asa-bans-law-firms-tiktok-adverts-for-misleading-consumers)

### Inferences
- **Safe zone (educational skit):** a chat between "Mia" and "Sam" about why depreciation is not cash, which ends on the Edlintics logo, with no character praising Edlintics or reporting a result from using it. This is an obviously fictional dramatization and not an endorsement under US or EU logic. A small "Fictional characters" note is good practice but probably not legally required.
- **Grey zone:** a character says "I learned this on Edlintics" or "Edlintics explains it well". It reads as an experience claim. Treat it as a testimonial-style claim: add an on-screen and spoken "Dramatization", and make the style obviously scripted (brand-styled interface, illustrated avatars, brand voice).
- **Red zone:** a realistic chat in which a "student" reports passing an exam, raising a grade or saving money thanks to the course, presented as a real screenshot. Under EU point 23c and the FTC rule, this works as a fake testimonial even with a "dramatization" tag, if the outcome claims imply real customer results Edlintics cannot substantiate. A disclaimer cannot cure an overall false impression, as the ASA TMS ruling shows. Only use real, documented customer quotes, with consent, for outcome claims.
- **Disclosure wording that fits the cited standards:** "Dramatization: fictional characters", shown on screen for the whole chat segment or at least at the start and at the praise moment, in readable contrast. Say it in the voiceover too if the video has one (Example 10 logic). A caption-only or hashtag-only disclosure is weak for video.
- **DSA:** the Digital Services Act mainly places obligations on platforms, such as ad repositories, a feature for users to declare commercial content, and dark-pattern rules. For Edlintics, the practical DSA-linked duty is to use each platform's branded-content or commercial-content toggle where relevant. The substantive fake-review ban comes from the UCPD, not the DSA. This is an inference: I did not fetch DSA text.

### Gaps
- I could not retrieve the official EUR-Lex text of Annex I points 23b and 23c or the Commission's 2021 UCPD Guidance Notice (C/2021/9320), because the pages returned empty. Wording above comes from secondary sources and the EC TRIS notification. Check before relying on exact wording.
- Not verified here: Omnibus penalties of at least 4% of annual turnover, or EUR 2 million where turnover data is unavailable, for widespread cross-border infringements. This is from background knowledge.
- No regulator decision found that specifically concerns fictional chat-interface ads.

## 2. Platform misleading-content rules (TikTok, Meta, YouTube)

### Takeaway
No major platform bans fictional or scripted chat videos as such. The rules target (a) realistic AI-generated or significantly edited media, which must be labelled, (b) harmful misinformation and impersonation, and (c) fake reviews and engagement. A scripted chat skit about accounting concepts is low risk. A fake screenshot that impersonates a real person or brand, or a fake customer review, can breach platform rules.

### Cited Findings
- **TikTok:** creators must "label AI-generated or significantly edited content" showing realistic scenes or people. "Unlabeled content may be removed, restricted, or labeled". Labels do not make harmful content acceptable. Banned "deceptive behaviors" include "impersonation, spam, fake reviews" — [TikTok Community Guidelines: Integrity and Authenticity](https://www.tiktok.com/community-guidelines/en/integrity-authenticity)
- TikTok's updated Community Guidelines took effect 13 Sep 2025. Labels can be TikTok's AI-generated label, a sticker or a caption. The guidelines ban harmful misinformation "no matter the intent", including "misleading AIGC" — [Partnership on AI case study](https://partnershiponai.org/TikTok-framework-case-study); [MediaPost](https://www.mediapost.com/publications/article/408219/tiktok-takes-clearer-stance-against-ai-misinformat.html); [Kolsquare](https://www.kolsquare.com/en/blog/what-are-tiktoks-new-rules-the-tiktok-updates-marketers-need-to-know)
- **Meta (Facebook, Instagram, Threads):** users must disclose with Meta's AI-disclosure tool when organic content has "photorealistic video or realistic-sounding audio" that was digitally created or altered, and penalties may apply. Meta has no formal satire or fiction exception, but notes that humour and satire are usually handled with context and labels rather than removal. Removal is focused on high-risk deception on matters of public importance — [Meta Misinformation policy](https://transparency.meta.com/policies/community-standards/misinformation/)
- **YouTube:** creators must disclose content that "seems realistic", including realistic scenes that never happened or a real person made to appear to say something they did not. No disclosure is needed for clearly unrealistic content, fully animated content, or minor edits. AI help with scripts and voice cloning of your own voice is also exempt. Repeated failure to disclose can lead to removal or suspension from the YouTube Partner Program — [YouTube Help: altered or synthetic content](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/14328491)

### Inferences
- A flat, brand-styled chat animation (bubbles, illustrated avatars, kinetic text) is "clearly unrealistic or animated" in YouTube's terms. It probably does not trigger mandatory AI labels on any of the three platforms. A photorealistic fake phone screen recording of a "real" conversation, or AI-generated realistic faces, moves toward the label-required zone.
- If AI voices (for example Kokoro) or AI faces are realistic, turn on the platform AI label. It costs little: YouTube says disclosure does not limit reach or monetization. That source covers YouTube only.
- Never stage a chat that impersonates a real person, a real school or exam board, or a real company, and never present a scripted chat as a leaked real one. That is where impersonation and misinformation rules apply.

### Gaps
- None of the platform pages fetched addresses fabricated chat screenshots specifically. The TikTok page I fetched was truncated, so separate TikTok rules on satire and fiction or on branded content were not checked.
- Not verified: the ad-policy (paid) versions of these rules, for example Meta and TikTok ad policies on testimonials and "before/after" claims. They are often stricter than organic rules and matter if these videos are boosted.

## 3. Imitating real messaging apps (iMessage, WhatsApp, Instagram DMs, Messenger)

### Takeaway
Brand policies from Apple and Meta forbid imitating their trade dress or using their marks in ways that imply affiliation or are misleading. Apple's ad policy also explicitly bars ads that mimic Apple's user interface. I found no published case or cease-and-desist letter against a fake-chat video creator, so enforcement risk for a small brand is low but not zero. The risk rises if the video is a paid ad or uses logos. An original "Edlintics-styled" chat interface (own colours, own bubble shapes, no app logos, no iOS status-bar chrome) avoids the issue almost entirely, and is the recommended default.

### Cited Findings
- **Apple:** third parties may not imitate "the distinctive Apple packaging, web site design, logos, or typefaces". Apple marks may not imply Apple's "affiliation with or endorsement, sponsorship, or support". Logos and icons need "an express written trademark license" for promotional materials — [Apple: Guidelines for Using Apple Trademarks](https://www.apple.com/legal/intellectual-property/guidelinesfor3rdparties.html)
- Apple's ad content policy: "Ads can't mimic or resemble the Apple brand or the user interface of Apple products." This rule governs ads on Apple's own ad platforms — [Apple Ads general content guidelines](https://ads.apple.com/apple-news/publishers/help/content-guidelines/0060-general-content-guidelines)
- **WhatsApp:** "you may not modify the WhatsApp Brand Resources in any way". The guidelines say not to make WhatsApp "the most distinctive or prominent feature of your materials", not to imply "partnership, sponsorship, or endorsement", and not to use the brand in a way that is "misleading". Use in TV or film requires review of the final cut before permission is given — [Meta: WhatsApp brand guidelines](https://www.meta.com/brand/resources/whatsapp/whatsapp-brand/)
- A vendor compliance guide says ads that break WhatsApp brand guidelines can be rejected — [Infobip WhatsApp compliance](https://www.infobip.com/docs/whatsapp/compliance)
- My searches found no reported Apple or Meta enforcement against fake-text-generator apps or fake-chat video makers. The only related App Store removal found was FakeMail (2011, email spoofing), where no trademark reason was given — [iphonesoft.fr](https://iphonesoft.fr/2011/03/05/fakemail-bientot-retire-de-l-app-store)

### Inferences
- **Copying iMessage's look closely** (blue and grey bubbles, iOS status bar, "Delivered" label, Apple fonts) or WhatsApp's look (green header, wallpaper pattern, ticks) uses distinctive trade dress in a commercial ad. It is low probability of enforcement but plainly outside both companies' published rules. Avoid it, especially in paid ads, where platform ad review may also reject it.
- **Logos:** never show app logos or app names in the interface, for example a "WhatsApp" header. That is trademark use with implied affiliation.
- **An original Edlintics chat design** avoids the issue: Edlintics blue and its own neutral bubbles, rounded shapes of Edlintics' own design, its own avatars, a generic or Edlintics-branded header, and no carrier, battery or iOS chrome. Generic chat conventions such as left and right bubbles and typing dots are not protectable by any single company. A distinctive own style also strengthens the "obviously fictional" reading from section 1.

### Gaps
- No case law found on trade dress in messaging UIs for third-party videos. Get lawyer review only if Edlintics ever wants to mimic a specific real app deliberately, for example as a parody format.
- Meta's general brand guidelines for Instagram and Messenger were not fetched. They are assumed similar to WhatsApp's, which is unverified.

## 4. Invented people: names, avatars, AI-generated faces

### Takeaway
Invented characters are fine. The risk comes from resemblance to a real, identifiable person, and from realistic AI faces, which carry new EU AI Act labelling duties from 2 Aug 2026. Use clearly illustrated avatars or initials, and common first names only with no surnames, schools or employers. Run a quick check that no character resembles a real public figure or a known student. If you use photorealistic AI faces, label them as AI-generated.

### Cited Findings
- A realistic AI face that resembles a real person can create liability even if no real photo was used; identifiability matters more than how the image was made. This source is a vendor blog, so treat it as advocacy — [Nightjar](https://nightjar.so/help-desk/can-i-be-sued-if-my-ai-fashion-model-resembles-a-celebrity-or-influencer)
- A generic, mock-up person is lower risk. A model reproducing a real person's likeness carries material risk — [Smart & Biggar](https://www.smartbiggar.ca/insights/publication/navigating-privacy-and-personality-rights-in-the-world-of-ai)
- Scarlett Johansson example: a disclaimer did not stop a synthetic "person" from being recognised as her, and legal action followed — [Brooklyn Law School](https://sports-entertainment.brooklaw.edu/film-tv/protecting-the-right-of-publicity-in-the-age-of-ai/)
- In the US, New York treats advertising uses as requiring consent, while California applies a transformative-use test. Courts usually apply the law of the person's home state — [Law360 via Eckert Seamans](https://www.eckertseamans.com/app/uploads/Law360_Schneider_Grammarly-Suit-Flags-Right-Of-Publicity-As-Key-AI-Issue.pdf)
- The FTC says using a celebrity's likeness via an AI avatar without permission can violate the reviews rule if consumers would think the celebrity endorsed the product — [FTC Q&A](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/consumer-reviews-testimonials-rule-questions-answers)
- **EU AI Act Article 50, applying from 2 Aug 2026:** deployers publishing deepfakes must disclose that content is AI-generated or manipulated. The Commission's draft guidelines (8 May 2026) read "deepfake" broadly. Intent to deceive is not required, and a "realistic synthetic depiction of a fictitious but natural-looking person" counts. Clearly unrealistic content generally falls outside. Evidently artistic, creative, satirical or fictional works get a reduced, not zero, disclosure duty. AI-generated text published to inform the public on matters of public interest also needs disclosure unless it has had genuine human editorial review. Penalties reach EUR 15 million or 3% of worldwide turnover. The guidelines are draft and non-binding, and a Code of Practice on labelling is being finalised — [Greenberg Traurig, June 2026](https://gtlaw.com/en/insights/2026/6/deepfakes-chatbots-ai-generated-text-european-commission-details-transparency-obligations-under-the-ai-act)
- AI Act disclosure does not cure personality-rights problems. A labelled lookalike can still infringe — [secondary commentary in search results](https://www.srd-rechtsanwaelte.de/en/blog/deepfake-labelling-under-the-ai-act-what-the-new-eu-guidance-clarifies)

### Inferences
- **Illustrated or flat avatars** (initials in a coloured circle, or the Ledger mascot style) are not "realistic" and fall outside AI Act deepfake labelling. They also make the skit obviously fictional, which helps with section 1. This is the lowest-risk option.
- **Photorealistic AI faces** in a chat profile picture fall within the draft Commission reading of Art. 50(4) when published by an EU deployer. Edlintics is EU-based, so add an "AI-generated" label: on screen, plus the platform AI toggle.
- **Names:** use common first names only ("Mia", "Leo"). Avoid full names, real school names, real lecturer names, real company names as employers, and the names of actual Edlintics students. Most of Hoang's 400+ former students come from identifiable schools, so check the character list against the client list (stored gitignored under data/contacts/) before publishing. Never reuse a real student's name or photo without written consent. That would also be a GDPR issue.
- **Lawyer review warranted:** before any campaign that uses real customer names, quotes or photos; before any paid ad that makes outcome claims ("pass rate", "grade up"); if a person complains that a character resembles them; or if Edlintics ever uses an AI face modelled on a real person.

### Gaps
- No EU-specific case law found on accidental name matches with fictional characters in ads. In civil-law countries, personality rights such as the German allgemeines Persönlichkeitsrecht or French droit à l'image generally require identifiability. This is background knowledge, not verified here.
- The final (non-draft) Commission Article 50 guidelines and Code of Practice were not confirmed as published as of this research.
- I did not verify whether the AI Omnibus delay to 2 Dec 2026 covers Art. 50(4) deployer duties or only Art. 50(2) provider marking. Greenberg Traurig ties the delay to 50(2) only.
