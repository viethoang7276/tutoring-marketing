## Prep pack — the six problems, ready to film

Each one: the **hook** you open with, the **numbers** to have on the spreadsheet, the **trap** (the
mid-video beat), and the **answer**. Currency is € (our market is Ireland/EU).

### CF-01 — Cash-flow statement (indirect)
**Hook:** "Profit was two million. The bank account went *down*. Both are correct — here's why."
**Set up on screen (€000s):**
- Net profit: **2,000**
- Add back depreciation: **+400**
- Increase in receivables: **(800)**  ← customers owe more, cash not in yet
- Increase in inventory: **(600)**
- Decrease in payables: **(300)**
- Purchase of equipment: **(1,500)**
- Dividends paid: **(200)**

**Solve:** Operating = 2,000 + 400 − 800 − 600 − 300 = **700**. Investing = **(1,500)**. Financing =
**(200)**. Net change in cash = 700 − 1,500 − 200 = **(1,000)**.
**The trap (mid-video):** the *sign* of working-capital changes. An **increase** in receivables is a
**use** of cash, so it's a minus — students flip it. And depreciation is added back because it never
left the bank, it's not "free money."
**Answer:** profit €2.0M, but cash **fell €1.0M**.

### TA-02 — T-accounts / double-entry
**Hook:** "A T-account isn't bookkeeping. It's a sentence with two halves that must agree."
**Transactions to record (€):**
1. Owner invests **10,000** cash
2. Buys equipment **4,000** cash
3. Buys inventory **2,000** on credit
4. Sells inventory (cost **1,200**) for **3,000** cash
5. Pays supplier **1,000**

**Solve:** draw Cash, Equipment, Inventory, Payables, Capital, Sales, COGS T-accounts; post each.
Prove total debits = total credits.
**The trap:** which side increases for each type — assets & expenses go up on the **debit**;
liabilities, equity & income go up on the **credit**. The credit purchase (#3) and recording COGS on
the sale (#4) are where people freeze.
**Answer:** trial balance ties; cash ends at 10,000 − 4,000 + 3,000 − 1,000 = **8,000**.

### AD-03 — Accruals vs deferrals
**Hook:** "You paid the cash in January. The expense belongs to March. The exam tests *that gap*."
**Two cases (€):**
- **Deferral:** pay **12,000** in January for a full year's insurance → expense **1,000 / month**.
  By end of March, expense recognised = **3,000**; prepaid asset still on the books = **9,000**.
- **Accrual:** you *earn* **5,000** of revenue in March but the cash arrives in May → recognise the
  revenue in **March** (receivable now), not when cash lands.

**The trap:** match to the **period the activity belongs to**, not the period the cash moves. Prepaid
= asset you've paid for but not used; accrued = earned/owed but not yet settled.
**Answer:** Q1 insurance expense 3,000 (not 12,000); March revenue 5,000 (despite €0 cash in March).

### DEP-04 — Depreciation
**Hook:** "Depreciation moves no money — and it's exactly where exams hide the trap."
**Numbers (€):** Equipment **50,000**, residual value **5,000**, useful life **5 years**,
straight-line.
**Solve:** annual depreciation = (50,000 − 5,000) / 5 = **9,000/yr**. Carrying value: Y1 41,000 →
Y2 32,000 → Y3 23,000 → Y4 14,000 → Y5 **5,000** (back to residual).
**The trap:** depreciation **reduces profit but moves no cash** — which is why it's *added back* in
the cash-flow statement (callback to CF-01). And residual value is part of the formula; students
depreciate the full 50,000.
**Answer:** €9,000 expense per year; €0 cash impact; ends at residual €5,000.

### DT-05 — Deferred tax
**Hook:** "Most students get deferred tax backwards. It turns on one word: *timing*."
**Numbers (€):** Year 1 — accounting depreciation **9,000** (straight-line, from DEP-04) vs tax
depreciation **15,000** (accelerated). Tax rate **25%**.
**Solve:** temporary difference = 15,000 − 9,000 = **6,000**. Deferred tax **liability** = 6,000 ×
25% = **1,500**. You got more tax relief now, so you'll pay more later → a liability today.
**The trap:** it's a **temporary (timing)** difference that reverses over the asset's life — not a
permanent one. Direction: faster tax relief now ⇒ deferred tax **liability** (not asset).
**Answer:** Year-1 deferred tax liability **€1,500**; it unwinds to zero across the asset's life.

### CON-06 — Consolidation
**Hook:** "Your textbook spends forty pages on consolidation. It's three steps."
**Numbers (€000s):** Parent buys **100%** of Sub for **800**; Sub's net assets at acquisition = **600**.
**Solve — three steps:**
1. **Add across** — combine the parent's and sub's assets and liabilities line by line.
2. **Eliminate** the parent's *Investment in Sub* (800) against the sub's equity at acquisition (600).
3. **Recognise goodwill** = 800 − 600 = **200** (the premium paid over net assets).

**The trap:** you don't just stack the two balance sheets — you must **cancel the investment against
the sub's equity**, or you double-count. (Mention: if it were 80% not 100%, the other 20% is
non-controlling interest — flag as the "advanced version" for a follow-up video.)
**Answer:** consolidated statement with **€200k goodwill**; investment line gone, equity = parent's only.
