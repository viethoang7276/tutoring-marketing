# Brilliant Instagram puzzle study: patterns for Edlintics

**Date:** 2026-10-22 · **Source:** instagram.com/brilliantorg profile grid (323 posts), viewed in Hoang's Chrome, scrolled from oldest to newest.
**Scope:** cover images only, roughly 300 posts seen, about 245 of them puzzle covers. Captions, answer slides and comments were not opened.
**Rights:** this is a pattern study. Questions are summarised in a few words to classify them; no question text, artwork or layout is copied for reuse. Edlintics puzzles stay original (see the `edlintics-puzzle-visuals` skill).

## 1. Five eras (oldest at the bottom of the grid)

| Era | Look | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Early | Saturated colour backgrounds (purple, teal, orange), white sans question, answer pills such as *Red / Blue / They're equal / Not enough info* | Heavy colour; diagrams flat and bold |
| 2. Classic | White background, serif (LaTeX-like) question top-centre, thin black line art, 1–2 accent colours, `?` in a coloured circle | The style in Hoang's screenshot. Clearest and most reusable |
| 3. Comic | Same white/serif look plus hand-drawn ink characters (scientists, kids, animals) acting out the problem | Characters illustrate the situation; they never carry the numbers |
| 4. "Problems" bar | Era 2 plus an orange footer bar: *Problems · logo · Difficulty* | Difficulty signalled on the card |
| 5. Current | App screenshots, creator reels, hiring and brand posts. **No puzzle covers** | Puzzles stopped. The feed now sells the product |

## 3. Ten recurring question archetypes

1. **Comparison:** "Which is larger / greater / brighter?" Options often include *They're equal* and *Not enough information*.
2. **Find the coloured unknown:** "What is the **blue** area?" The coloured word in the question matches the colour on the diagram, and a `?` sits on the unknown.
3. **Count it:** "How many triangles / paths / colours?"
4. **Choose the order or setup:** "Add cream now or later?", "Which arrangement puts out more light?" (closest match to P21-02).
5. **Prediction against intuition:** "Which ball hits first?", "Heat or cool the nut?", "Which ice cream melts first?"
6. **Estimate (Fermi):** "How close to Everest with Lego?", "CO2 exhaled per day?"
7. **Logic from statements:** truth-tellers, the four-card verification task, "How many statements can be true?"
8. **Symbol puzzles:** cryptarithms, balance scales of shapes, operation trees with a mystery number.
9. **Scaling:** "How much bigger is the 2V bubble?", "Elephant ear area at twice the weight?"
10. **Probability intuition:** "More likely than not?", "Which robot is more likely to end in its box?"

## 4. Visual grammar worth adopting

- **One question, top-centre, about 6–15 words**, usually starting *What / Which / How many / Can you*.
- **Colour-coded keyword:** one word in the question is set in the same colour as the unknown on the diagram ("the **green** angle"). It links text and picture without a legend.
- **`?` marker** in a small coloured circle on the unknown.
- **Givens written on the drawing** (numbers on edges, areas inside shapes). No separate legend.
- **Assumptions as 1–2 short numbered lines** under the diagram ("1. The hexagon is regular. 2. The large triangle's area is 90."), or a one-line *Hint:* in small type. This is how Brilliant meets the "all assumptions visible" bar without a text box.
- **Answer options**, when used, are small circled letters (A) (B) (C) (D) or pills in a single row.
- **White space:** the diagram takes most of the card; the logo is small at the bottom.
- **Style:** thin ink outlines, flat fills, 1–2 accents. Characters, when used, act out the scenario and are drawn in ink.
- **Trap design:** almost every card offers a tempting wrong answer that follows from a common misconception (equal totals mean equal outcomes, faster means better, bigger means more).

## 5. Incidental engagement signals (hover counts, not systematic)

Only a few cards were hovered while scrolling, so treat this as anecdote, not evidence. The highest comment counts were on comparison or will-it-work cards with an obvious tempting answer: circles fitting a parallelogram (≈3.1k likes, 496 comments), a dot-pattern formula (≈3.1k, 226), a mystery-number tree (≈2.5k, 306), and a square-root equation (≈1.8k, 302). Pure geometry-angle cards drew fewer comments (≈300–600 likes, 40–95 comments). Edlintics's own results are what counts.

## 6. Mapping archetypes to original Edlintics puzzles (ideas, not drafts)

| Brilliant archetype | Business version to research |
|---|---|
| Comparison with "They're equal" | Markup vs margin on the same price; arithmetic vs compounded average return |
| Choose the order (tea and cream) | Job sequencing, as in P21-02; FIFO vs LIFO when prices rise |
| Balance scales of shapes | The accounting equation: what keeps assets = liabilities + equity in balance |
| Operation tree / mystery number | DuPont ROE chain; compounding chain |
| Sampling bias (average of 6 fish) | Survivorship bias in fund returns |
| Mean = median puzzle | Salary or price distributions; what a reported average hides |
| Limiting reactant | Bottleneck or constraint in a process |
| Truth-teller statements / card verification | Audit assertions; confirmation bias in testing a claim |
| Stable matching | Two-sided markets and matching |
| Scaling (2V bubble) | Fixed vs variable cost as volume doubles |

## 7. Implications for the current trial

- Keep the two-slide format. Brilliant's covers carry the question only; the answer comes later.
- Add the **colour-coded keyword** and the `?` marker to Edlintics cards.
- Replace text-box assumptions with **1–2 numbered lines** under the diagram, and draw the rest into the picture.
- Treat era 2 (white, serif, line art) as the closest reference for Edlintics. Avoid era 5, which is product marketing, not puzzles.
